## 📚 Key Learnings from This Analysis

### 1. Title vs. Requirements Mismatch is a Pay Trap
When a job title (Junior) doesn't match the experience requirements (5+ years) or responsibilities (architect, mentor, lead meetings), it's almost always intentional. Companies save 30–50% on salary by downgrading titles while keeping expectations senior-level. **Always cross-check title, experience ask, and responsibilities as a trio.**

### 2. Benefits Language Reveals Culture More Than Perks Do
Real benefits are concrete: salary band, number of PTO days, health coverage, parental leave. When the "benefits" section is filled with cultural phrases ("fast-paced", "like a family", "thrive under pressure"), it means there's nothing concrete to list. **Vague culture language is a proxy for missing compensation.**

### 3. "Remote" Must Be Verified in the Location Clause
The word "remote" in a job title means nothing without checking the full location section. Phrases like "relocation may be required", "hybrid after onboarding", or "must be able to travel" buried in fine print are bait-and-switch structures. **Always read the Location line fully, not just the job title.**

### 4. Early-Stage Startup Signals Need a Financial Lens
A startup's age, team size, and funding status are public risk factors — not just vibes. An 8-month-old company with no public funding and employee reviews already citing overwork is statistically much more likely to shut down or go through a crunch than one with disclosed Series A funding. **Ask about runway as directly as you ask about salary.**

### 5. The Aggregate Pattern Matters More Than Any Single Flag
None of these flags alone would be disqualifying. "Fast-paced culture" at a funded Series B startup is fine. "No salary disclosed" at a large established company might mean "negotiate." It's when **multiple flags cluster in the same posting** that the pattern becomes diagnostic. Train yourself to read JDs holistically, not line by line.

### 6. Interview Questions Are a Candidate's Due Diligence Tool
Candidates often treat interviews as a test they're taking. They're not — they're a bilateral evaluation. Asking direct questions about on-call structure, relocation triggers, funding runway, and retention is professional, not aggressive. **Companies that react negatively to due diligence questions are demonstrating exactly the culture the JD hinted at.**
